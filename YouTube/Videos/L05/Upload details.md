## L05 – the long video

**Schedule:** Wednesday 11 November 2026, **17:00 Central European Time** (21:30 in Sri Lanka). Time zone: (GMT+01:00) Berlin.

**Title**

```
Who Works the Longest Hours in Europe? (It's Not Who You Think)
```

Title B (Test & compare, with thumbnail B):

```
Germans Work the Fewest Hours in Europe. Here's Why.
```

**Description**

```
The hours a worker actually puts in over a year, across 31 European countries. Croatia and Greece come top, Germany comes last. Why part-time work, holidays and shorter weeks explain the German number, how the working year has shrunk since 1970 (and since 1870), and whether long hours pay.

Chapters

0:29 What we're measuring
1:05 The 2023 ranking
2:53 Since 1970
4:17 Since 1870
5:03 Why Germany is last
5:46 Does it pay?
6:29 Long hours, low pay?
6:57 The US and Asia
7:48 What it means

Sources
- Penn World Table (2025), via Our World in Data: annual working hours per worker, 1970–2023, and GDP per hour worked, 2023. https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/annual-working-hours-per-worker
- Huberman and Minns (2005), Hours of Work in Old and New Worlds: The Long View, 1870–2000, via Our World in Data: hours in 1870.
- OECD, GDP per hour worked: used to check the gap in output per hour.
What we did to the data: hours are the average actually worked per worker in a year, including overtime and part-time workers. Countries collect this differently, so small gaps are not meaningful. The 1870 figures cover full-time workers outside farming only, so they don't compare exactly with today's. The ranking covers the 27 EU countries plus the UK, Norway, Switzerland and Iceland. Output per hour is in 2021 international dollars, adjusted for prices.
```
